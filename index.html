Circuits that remember

Everything you have built so far has the memory of a goldfish. Take the inputs away and an AND gate forgets instantly — it only ever knows about right now. But your phone remembers your wallpaper after you turn it off and on. Caps Lock stays on after you let go of the key. Something in there is holding on. This topic is about the moment a circuit stops reacting and starts remembering, and it is the biggest step in the whole unit.

How can a circuit possibly remember?

This is the question worth getting properly. Gates have no storage in them. No little box, no battery, nowhere to write anything down. And yet two of them wired a certain way will hold a value for years. Here is the trick.

The rumour that keeps itself alive

You tell Shanice something. Shanice tells Kemoy. Kemoy tells you. Now you are hearing it from Kemoy, so you tell Shanice again — and round it goes. Nobody is storing the rumour anywhere. There is no notebook. It survives purely because it keeps arriving back where it started. That loop is the memory. A latch does exactly this with two gates: each one's output is wired into the other one's input, so each gate keeps telling the other what it already believes. Cut the loop and the memory evaporates instantly, which is precisely why RAM forgets the moment the current goes.

Take a latch apart

Two NOR gates, each one feeding the other. Step through it and watch the loop lock itself in place.

The feedback wire is the entire invention. Everything else on this page — SR, D, JK, T, registers, RAM, the save button — is that one loop with extra controls bolted onto it. If you understand why the loop holds, you understand sequential circuits. If you skip it and try to memorise four tables instead, this topic will feel impossible, and that is not your fault; it is the wrong order.
Two names you now need. Everything up to topic 3 was a combinational circuit: output depends only on the inputs right now, no history, fully described by a truth table. From here on we are building sequential circuits: output depends on the inputs and on what happened before. That is why a plain truth table is no longer enough, and why we need characteristic tables and timing diagrams instead.

The clock, and why edges matter

A latch changes the instant its inputs change, which sounds fine until you have a million of them and they all start updating at slightly different moments. So we put everything on a schedule.

Sports day, and nobody moves before the whistle

Forty children on a start line. Some are faster, some are still tying a lace, one is looking at a bird. If they each go whenever they feel ready, the race is chaos and the result is meaningless. So there is a whistle. Nobody moves until it blows, and then everybody moves at once. A clock signal is that whistle, blowing a few billion times a second, and the crucial part is this: what matters is the moment the whistle sounds, not how long the sound lasts. That moment is called an edge, and almost everything in this topic hangs off it.

Triggers on
Level-triggered versus edge-triggered, and the mark it costs. A latch is level-triggered: while the clock is high it keeps watching its inputs, and anything that wobbles during that window gets through. A flip-flop is edge-triggered: it takes one snapshot at the instant the clock flips, and ignores everything else entirely. That single difference is the whole reason we bother building flip-flops out of latches — and "state the difference between a latch and a flip-flop" is an exam question that comes round like a birthday.

The four flip-flops

Same loop underneath, four different sets of controls on the front. Click through them, flip the switches, then pulse the clock and watch what changes — and, just as importantly, what does not.

Four remote controls for the same television

SR is the old remote with separate ON and OFF buttons, and if you press both at once it sulks. D is the remote with a single dial: whatever you set it to is what you get, no arguments. JK is the fancy one where pressing both buttons together does something genuinely useful instead of breaking. T has a single button that just flips between on and off, like the light switch in your bedroom. Same TV every time. Four ways of driving it, each convenient for a different job.

Why four, and which one for which job

They are not four difficulty levels of the same thing. Each one was designed to make a specific job either safe or effortless, and picking the wrong one is a real design mistake, not just a wrong answer on a test.

Built two ways: NOR, or NAND

The latch underneath every flip-flop on this page was drawn with NOR gates. It works exactly as well built from NAND gates instead — CAPE likes to ask for both, and the one thing that changes is which level does the job.

Same latch, opposite personality. The NOR version idles with S = R = 0 and a 1 does the setting or resetting — inputs are normally low, and asserting one means driving it high. The NAND version idles with S′ = R′ = 1 and a 0 does the work — inputs are normally high, and asserting one means pulling it low. That is why NAND versions of this circuit are always drawn with the inputs named S′ and R′: the prime is warning you they are active-low. Feed a NAND latch's inputs 0 and 0 together and you get the exact same contradiction as feeding a NOR latch's inputs 1 and 1 — same disease, opposite symptom.

What's actually inside the box

The D, JK and T symbols you were just clicking through are not new inventions. Every one of them is the same NOR latch from the last section with a small amount of extra gating bolted onto the front — and that extra gating is exactly what fixes the SR latch's invalid state. Pick a type below.

Timing diagrams

This is the bit that costs people marks, and it is not because it is hard. It is because nobody shows them a method. Here is one, and then you are going to draw eight of them yourself.

A timing diagram is just a heart monitor for a wire

You have seen the screen beside a hospital bed: a line crawling left to right, high when something is happening, low when it is not. A timing diagram is exactly that, except there is one line per wire, all stacked up and sharing the same left-to-right time axis. Nothing about it is clever. The only skill is reading down the page at one moment in time instead of reading along it.

The method. Four steps, and do not improvise.

  1. Mark the edges first. Before you look at any input, draw a faint vertical line down the whole diagram at every active clock edge. If it is a rising-edge device, that is every 0→1. Those lines are the only moments anything is allowed to happen.
  2. At each line, read the inputs going down. Not what they were doing a moment before, not what they are about to do. What they are at that instant. This is where most marks are lost.
  3. Apply the rule once. D copies. T flips if T is 1. JK sets, resets, toggles or holds. SR sets, resets or holds. One decision per line.
  4. Draw Q flat until the next line. Q is not allowed to move between edges. If your Q wiggles somewhere in the middle of a cycle, you have made a mistake, guaranteed.
The one that catches everybody. An input can change six times during a clock cycle and the flip-flop will not care about any of it. Only the value sitting on the input at the instant of the edge counts. Examiners know this, which is why exam inputs wiggle around so much — they are checking whether you are reading the edges or just eyeballing the shape.

Putting them in a row

One flip-flop holds one bit, which is not much use. Line up eight and clock them together and you have something a computer can actually work with.

Eight sticky notes and one pen stroke

One sticky note holds one thing. Fine. But if you line up eight of them and write across all eight at exactly the same instant, you have captured something with real content — a number, a letter, an address. The trick is not the notes. It is that they all get written simultaneously, because they all share one clock. That is a register, and it is why the clock wire goes to every flip-flop rather than each one having its own.

Ripple counter

Four T flip-flops, each one clocked by the one before it. Each stage halves the frequency, and the whole thing counts in binary without being told how.

Nobody designed a counter. It fell out. A T flip-flop with T tied to 1 flips on every pulse, so its output runs at half the speed of its clock. Feed that output into the next flip-flop's clock and you get a quarter speed. Then an eighth. Line up four and read the outputs as a binary number and — without anybody writing a single line of counting logic — it counts 0, 1, 2, 3, all the way to 15 and back to 0. Halving is counting. Sit with that one for a second.
Why it is called a "ripple" counter, and why that is a problem. Each stage has to wait for the one before it to flip, so a change ripples along the chain exactly like the carry in topic 3 did. For a moment in the middle, the outputs show a number that was never meant to exist. Fine for blinking an LED, dangerous for anything reading it. The fix is a synchronous counter where every flip-flop shares one clock — more gates, no glitches, same trade you have now met three times.

Lab guide

Eight builds in Logic.ly or CircuitVerse, in the order that makes each one easier than the last — starting with the two that use nothing but bare NOR and NAND gates, before you ever touch a pre-built flip-flop symbol. Read the pin section first — it is the reason most lab circuits sit there doing nothing.

Read this before your circuit refuses to work

Every flip-flop in the simulator has two extra pins you did not ask for: PRE′ and CLR′. They override everything else, instantly, without waiting for the clock. And here is the part that catches everyone: they are active LOW. That little prime mark means they do their job when you feed them a 0.

The fire alarm beside the light switch

Your classroom has light switches, and it also has a fire alarm. The alarm does not wait its turn, does not care what the switches are doing, and overrides the entire room the instant it goes off. PRE′ and CLR′ are that alarm. Leave them alone and the room works normally. Trip one by accident and nothing else you do matters.

Nine times out of ten, one of them is floating or wired to 0, and your circuit is being held down by an alarm you forgot you installed. Both to toggle switches, both switched ON, every single time \u2014 unless the lab specifically asks you to wire CLR′ to a push button as a reset.
